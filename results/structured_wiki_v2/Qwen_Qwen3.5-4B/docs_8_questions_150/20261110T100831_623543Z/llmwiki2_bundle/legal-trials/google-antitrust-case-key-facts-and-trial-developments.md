The U.S. government's antitrust trial against Google stems from a 2020 claim regarding its dominance in the online search market, separate from a January filing by the Department of Justice and eight states to halt alleged anticompetitive schemes. The government argues Google uses platforms and deals to block competition in search or advertising, hindering competitors from accessing necessary data. Google defends its tactics as legal business practices, asserting that being the default engine is desirable rather than illegal.

During the trial, Google CEO Sundar Pichai testified, defending tactics including deals to make Google the default search engine. Google search head Prabhakar Raghavan also testified on October 28, revealing that Google spent $26.3 billion in 2021 to make itself the default search engine across platforms, including attempts to preinstall Chrome on iPhones. Of this total, approximately $18 billion went directly to Apple, with the deal in place since 2003. Pichai stated the decision to pay for default status was made with the consumer in mind, to gather data and be the best search engine across devices.

Pichai admitted in 2018 attempting to get Tim Cook to preinstall Google on every iOS device to connect services inseparably, hoping for a win-win where Google gains search users/data and Apple gains revenue from a search agreement, but Cook declined as Apple does not preload third-party software. Additionally, the DOJ accused Google of systematically destroying chats via a history-off option deleting messages every 24 hours; Pichai claimed he took action against the history-off default in February to comply with the DOJ's litigation hold, though a 2021 message exchange showed Pichai asked to turn off history for a personnel matter unrelated to the hold.

Historical context includes a 2007 Google product strategy meeting email showing that when users changed their browser homepage to Google, they did 15% more Google searches, dropping 27% when they switched away. In 2005, Microsoft made Internet Explorer the default search engine, prompting Google's legal chief David Drummond to send an angry letter accusing this of anticompetitive behavior. Bellshaw used Drummond's letter to establish Google's hypocrisy regarding defaults, noting Drummond argued problems are worsened by how defaults change and most users do not change them, arguments now used by DuckDuckGo, Brave, and Bing against Google.

The case is expected to continue for several weeks, potentially resulting in a Microsoft-like outcome where Google must change behavior, share APIs, and be banned from exclusive deals. Google might have to turn over data collected daily (16 times more than Bing) to other search engines, with ripple effects on FTC cases against Amazon, Apple, and Facebook regarding monopolies and app sales.
