The U.S. government's antitrust trial against Google stems from a 2020 claim over its dominance in the online search market, separate from a January filing by the Department of Justice and eight states to halt its anticompetitive scheme. The government argues Google uses platforms and deals to block competition in search or advertising, hindering competitors from accessing necessary data. Google CEO Sundar Pichai testified, defending tactics including deals to make Google the default search engine, stating that being the default is a desirable business practice, not illegal. 

Google search head Prabhakar Raghavan testified that Google spent $26.3 billion in 2021 to make itself the default search engine across platforms, including attempts to preinstall Chrome on iPhones. Approximately $18 billion of this total went directly to Apple, with the deal in place since 2003. Pichai claimed the decision to pay for default status was made with the consumer in mind to gather data and improve the search engine. A 2007 internal email revealed that when users changed their browser homepage to Google, they performed 15% more searches, dropping 27% when they switched away.

Regarding preinstallation, Pichai admitted in 2018 to attempting to convince Tim Cook to preinstall Google services on every iOS device to create an inseparable connection between Google and Apple services, though Tim Cook refused. Google also faced accusations in February of systematically destroying internal chat messages despite a litigation hold, though Pichai later took action to change the chat history default to comply. 

Legal arguments highlighted hypocrisy, noting that while Google denounced Microsoft's 2005 default search engine monopoly, Google's own actions worsened the problem. The DOJ argued that changes to defaults worsen issues, as most users do not change defaults, and noted that competitors like DuckDuckGo, Brave, and Bing use similar arguments against Google's deals with Apple. The case is expected to continue for several weeks and is considered the biggest tech antitrust fight since the U.S. trial against Microsoft in the 1990s. If ruled against Google, the company may be required to change behavior and share APIs with third-party developers.
