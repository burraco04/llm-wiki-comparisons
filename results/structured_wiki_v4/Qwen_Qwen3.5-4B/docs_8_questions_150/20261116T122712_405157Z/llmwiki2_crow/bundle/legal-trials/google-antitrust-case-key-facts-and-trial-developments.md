The U.S. government's antitrust trial against Google stems from a 2020 claim regarding its dominance in the online search market, separate from a January filing by the Department of Justice and eight states aiming to halt Google's anticompetitive scheme. The government argues Google uses platforms and deals to block competition in search or advertising, hindering competitors from accessing data needed to improve products. Google argues its actions are legal business practices because being the default engine is desirable, not illegal.

During the trial, Google CEO Sundar Pichai testified, defending tactics including deals with Apple and partners to make Google the default search engine. Google search head Prabhakar Raghavan testified on October 28 that Google spent $26.3 billion in 2021 to make itself the default search engine across platforms, including attempts to preinstall Chrome on iPhones. About $18 billion of the $26.3 billion total went directly to Apple; Google's deal with Apple has been in place since 2003. Pichai stated the decision to pay for defaults was made with the consumer in mind, to gather data and be the best search engine across devices. A 2007 Google product strategy meeting email showed that when users changed their browser homepage to Google, they did 15% more Google searches, dropping 27% when they switched away.

Pichai admitted in 2018 attempting to get Tim Cook to preinstall Google on every iOS device to create inseparable services, hoping for a win-win where Google gains search users/data and Apple gains revenue from a search agreement, but Cook declined as Apple does not preload third-party software. In February, the DOJ accused Google of systematically destroying chats via a history-off option deleting messages every 24 hours; Pichai claimed he took action against the history-off default in February to comply with the DOJ's litigation hold, though Bellshaw cited a 2021 message exchange where Pichai discussed turning off history for a personnel matter unrelated to the hold. In 2005, Microsoft made Internet Explorer the default search engine, prompting Google's legal chief David Drummond to send an angry letter accusing this of anticompetitive behavior; Bellshaw used Drummond's letter to highlight Google's hypocrisy regarding defaults, noting that changes to defaults worsen problems and most users do not change them, arguments also used by DuckDuckGo, Brave, and Bing against Google.

The case is expected to continue for several weeks, potentially resulting in a Microsoft-like outcome where Google must change behavior, share APIs, and be banned from exclusive deals; Google might have to turn over collected data to other search engines, given the DOJ noted Google gets 16 times more data than Bing daily, with ripple effects on FTC cases against Amazon, Apple, and Facebook.
